Abstract

Large rectified-flow image generators such as FLUX and Z-Image deliver state-of-the-art quality, but their sampler advances every latent coordinate under a single global time. We argue that this uniform schedule is wasteful: at any intermediate noise level some image regions are already structurally settled while others still carry most of their stochasticity, and the settled regions would benefit the unsettled ones if allowed to advance further.

We introduce ITPV, an inference-time, training-free method that assigns each latent coordinate its own denoising speed. ITPV generalises adaptive Layer Normalization to accept a per-token time map, reusing the pretrained time embedder and modulation weights without architectural changes. At every step it constructs a single speculative future latent through a Tweedie-style extrapolation, queries the model once at this accelerated time, and grades each coordinate by the cosine similarity between its current and speculative velocities. Coordinates whose direction would not change after the jump are advanced aggressively; coordinates whose direction would change stay on the canonical schedule.

The result is a strict generalisation of standard sampling: it recovers the canonical trajectory exactly in the limit of zero per-coordinate speedup, requires no caching or profiling, and integrates with classifier-free guidance through a single extra forward pass per step. On FLUX.2-Klein-4B-Base, Z-Image, and Stable Diffusion 3, ITPV at 16 sampling steps matches the perceptual quality of 50-step classifier-free guidance, as confirmed by automatic distribution, quality, and diversity metrics on 30K MS-COCO captions and by the GenEval2 compositional benchmark. At a matched compute budget, ITPV improves over every training-free accelerator we compare against, including state-of-the-art samplers, caching, and region-adaptive methods. We further validate our results with a blinded pairwise user study with 14 participants on two of the backbones.

Method

One denoising time per coordinate

Standard samplers advance all $N$ spatial tokens by the same $\Delta t$ at every step. But denoising is spatially heterogeneous: low-frequency layout and flat areas settle early, while texture, edges and salient subjects keep changing. A region whose velocity at time $t$ and at a hypothetical future time $t' < t$ point in nearly the same direction can safely jump ahead; a region whose direction would change cannot. ITPV replaces the scalar time $t$ with a time map $\mathbf{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N}$ and lets each coordinate move at its own pace.

01
adaLN is a pointwise hook
Diffusion transformers condition on time through adaptive LayerNorm, whose modulation network is a linear map over the last dimension. Feeding a per-token time map through the existing time embedder yields per-token scale and gate fields by plain broadcasting: no new parameters, no architectural change.
02
A closed-form speculative latent
From the current velocity, a Tweedie-style noise estimate $\hat\epsilon = \mathbf{z}_\mathbf{T} - (\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{T})\,v^+$ gives a latent at an accelerated time $\mathbf{T}^{\text{future}}_\rho = \mathbf{1} - \rho(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{T})$ that is native to that time, so one extra forward pass probes the future velocity $v^{\text{future}}_\rho$.
03
A per-coordinate speedup rule
The cosine similarity $c_i$ between current and future velocities is mapped to a speedup $s_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(-\log(1-c_i)/\lambda,\,0,\,1\right)$, which blends the canonical step with the speculative jump: $\Delta = (\mathbf{1}-s)\odot d\mathbf{T} + s\odot d\mathbf{T}_\rho$.
Method Diagram Open ↗

The ITPV method in five stages. (1) The latent $\mathbf{z}$ and the per-token time map $\mathbf{T}$ are combined through adaLN and passed through the model to produce the velocity $v$. (2) The velocity is used to form a Tweedie estimate of a future latent $\mathbf{z}^{\text{future}}$ at a future time $\mathbf{T}^{\text{future}}$. (3) The future latent and time are passed through the model to get an estimated future velocity $v^{\text{future}}$. (4) The per-coordinate speedup $s$ is computed from the cosine similarity between $v$ and $v^{\text{future}}$. (5) $s$ determines how far to advance $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{z}$.

A strict generalisation. When every entry of $\mathbf{T}$ is equal, per-token adaLN collapses to the standard global adaLN, and when $s = 0$ the update is exactly the canonical Euler step with classifier-free guidance. ITPV needs no offline profiling, no feature cache and no trajectory statistics. It costs one extra forward pass per step (three instead of two with CFG), which is paid back once the step count drops by more than a factor of $3/2$; in practice we go from 50 canonical steps to 16. The only hyperparameters are the speculative speedup $\rho$ and the log-scale temperature $\lambda$, fixed to $(\rho, \lambda) = (1.5, 0.5)$ across all backbones and prompts.

Blinded user study

Automatic metrics correlate imperfectly with human judgement in low-NFE regimes, so we ran a blinded pairwise study on FLUX.2-Klein-4B-Base and Z-Image. Each participant saw an ITPV@16 image and a baseline image with the same prompt and seed, and rated overall quality and prompt alignment independently (A, B, or tie), collecting 1,402 valid ratings per axis from 14 participants.

ITPV wins the majority of decided pairs against every baseline on both axes, with significant preferences over TaylorSeer, TORS and FoCa. Against the full-budget CFG@50 reference neither axis reaches significance: ITPV at 16 steps (48 NFEs) is indistinguishable from CFG@50 (100 NFEs) despite using less than half the compute.

Analysis

Two knobs, one operating point

ITPV has only two hyperparameters: the speculative speedup $\rho > 1$ and the log-scale temperature $\lambda > 0$. They are not separable: a small $\lambda$ makes the speedup saturate, so it must be paired with a small jump $\rho$, while a larger $\lambda$ pulls the speedup back towards the canonical schedule and tolerates a larger jump. The best $\rho$ therefore grows with $\lambda$, performance degrades off-diagonal, and the maximum is our operating point $(\rho, \lambda) = (1.5, 0.5)$, used unchanged on every backbone.

Granularity matters. Holding the probe, the cosine blend and the NFE budget fixed and varying only the resolution of the time map (one global time, square block grids, or content-defined regions borrowed from RAS and SDiT), the full per-token map is best on every metric, showing that per-token time is itself a key driver of quality rather than a side effect of the probe.
